Why Your Slot Promotions Aren't Delivering Real Growth

Higher GGR doesn't always mean growth. Learn how slot studios can measure promotional ROI, improve casino lobby positioning and grow operator revenue.

Thomas Rhys Jones·19 November 2026
Share on LinkedIn ↗

More spins, higher GGR and a successful tournament don't necessarily mean you've grown your business. Here's how slot studios and operators should think about promotional performance.

There's a familiar conversation in iGaming account management.

A studio runs a promotion with an operator. Games receive better positioning, players participate in a tournament, and GGR increases during the campaign.

The promotion ends. The account manager puts together a report showing increased activity, everyone congratulates themselves, and attention shifts to the next campaign.

But there's a fairly important question missing.

Did the promotion actually generate additional revenue, or did it simply move existing revenue around?

It's a distinction that matters enormously, particularly in an industry where studios compete for limited promotional space and operators have thousands of games available to them.

Activity is not the same as growth

Let's imagine a slot studio generates €100,000 in monthly GGR with an operator.

It agrees a promotional campaign, secures prominent lobby positioning and provides a prize pool.

During the campaign, GGR rises to €130,000.

A 30% increase. Excellent result, right?

Possibly.

But what if the operator's overall casino revenue stayed flat? What if most participating players simply moved from other games into the promoted titles? What if the studio funded €20,000 in incentives, and performance returned to its original level as soon as the promotion ended?

The studio has increased its share of the operator's revenue, which may itself be commercially valuable. But it hasn't necessarily generated additional casino revenue.

And the studio's own financial return may be considerably less attractive than the headline suggests.

These are different outcomes, and they need to be measured differently.

These figures are illustrative, not results from a particular operator or studio.

Stop measuring campaigns in isolation

Most account managers have access to some combination of turnover, GGR, active players, rounds played and average bet.

They're useful measures. But looking at them without context can lead to some fairly poor commercial decisions.

I would start with five questions:

1. What would performance have looked like without the promotion?

Compare results against a credible baseline, not simply the previous week. Seasonality, player activity, game launches and other operator campaigns can all influence performance.

Where possible, use controlled tests or comparable groups to establish whether the campaign genuinely changed behaviour.

2. Did the promotion attract new players or activate existing ones?

An increase in unique players is encouraging, but understanding their previous activity matters.

Were these players discovering the studio's games for the first time? Returning after a period of inactivity? Or simply shifting from another title they already enjoyed?

Those distinctions change the commercial value of the result.

3. What happened to the rest of the portfolio?

A promoted title might deliver a fantastic increase while other games from the same studio decline.

If the campaign effectively transfers revenue between titles, the supplier's overall position may barely change.

Measure the whole portfolio, not just the headline game.

4. What did the campaign actually cost?

Prize pools, free spins, promotional contributions, commercial discounts and other incentives all affect profitability.

And remember that a studio's revenue share is not the same as the operator's GGR.

Both parties need to understand the economics from their own perspective.

5. What happened after the promotion ended?

This might be the most important question.

Did the campaign create sustained player interest? Did the games maintain improved performance? Did players continue returning without additional incentives?

Or did everything revert to normal as soon as the promotional support disappeared?

Short-term activity is relatively easy to generate. Sustainable commercial improvement is harder.

Lobby positioning should be a commercial discussion

Every slot studio wants better visibility in an operator's casino lobby.

It's understandable. You can develop an excellent game, secure distribution, complete the integration and still generate disappointing results if players struggle to find it.

But there's a difference between asking for better positioning and presenting a credible commercial argument for why an operator should provide it.

An operator has limited premium space and plenty of suppliers competing for attention.

Why should your games get priority?

Because you've got a new release? So has everybody else.

Because your studio has a promotional budget? That's useful, but hardly a compelling strategy on its own.

A stronger approach is to demonstrate what the operator stands to gain.

For example, you might identify an underrepresented game category, show how similar content has performed with comparable audiences, or propose a controlled placement test with clearly defined success criteria.

The conversation changes from:

Can we get a homepage banner for two weeks?

To:

We believe this content can improve performance with a specific player segment. Here's why, here's the support we'll provide, and here's how we propose measuring the result.

One is a request for a favour.

The other is a commercial proposition.

The operator's success matters as much as the studio's

This is where I think supplier account management can become considerably more valuable.

It's easy to become fixated on your own revenue targets.

But operators aren't there simply to distribute a supplier's games. They're managing their own commercial objectives, player experience, promotional budgets, compliance obligations and competing priorities.

A studio might be delighted to increase its share of an operator's GGR from 2% to 3%.

The operator may be rather less excited if the increase comes entirely from players switching away from other games, without improving overall contribution.

That doesn't automatically make the campaign unsuccessful. Supplier diversification, a more compelling portfolio and better player engagement can all justify promotional investment.

But you need to understand what success looks like for both parties.

The best commercial partnerships are built around shared objectives, not competing spreadsheets.

That means involving casino operations, CRM, product and marketing stakeholders where appropriate, rather than treating promotions as something arranged exclusively between two account managers.

It also means respecting responsible gambling requirements and local promotional restrictions. Commercial ambition doesn't override either.

Build a repeatable promotional process

None of this needs to involve a sophisticated analytics platform or an enormous data team.

I'd begin with a relatively simple framework.

Before the campaign

Agree the commercial objective, the target audience, the expected contribution and the baseline against which results will be assessed.

Document the promotional costs and commitments from both sides.

Critically, agree how success will be measured before anyone knows the outcome.

During the campaign

Monitor player participation, game launches, rounds, turnover and GGR, but also look at performance across the wider portfolio.

Where data permits, assess exposure and conversion by placement, device, market or relevant player segment.

Don't mistake normal short-term GGR volatility for proof that a campaign is working.

After the campaign

Review performance against the baseline and, ideally, an appropriate control group.

Look at portfolio-level contribution, net economics and subsequent player behaviour.

Then decide what should change next time.

Was the promotional mechanic effective? Did a particular category perform better than expected? Was the chosen lobby position worth its opportunity cost? Would a different audience or smaller incentive have produced a better return?

Most importantly, turn the findings into the next commercial decision.

A campaign report that doesn't influence future activity is largely an administrative exercise.

Better account management creates better revenue

There is a broader point here.

The role of a supplier account manager shouldn't simply be to arrange promotions, discuss new releases and deal with operational issues.

Those things matter, but they aren't the full job.

Strong account management means understanding where revenue is coming from, identifying opportunities to improve performance, challenging assumptions and helping partners make better commercial decisions.

It means recognising when a particular activity isn't working, even if the numbers initially look encouraging.

It also means knowing when not to spend money.

Sometimes an operator doesn't need another tournament or another incentive. Perhaps the existing content needs better categorisation, a more targeted audience or simply greater visibility.

Perhaps the studio needs to focus its attention on a completely different account where the potential return is higher.

Good commercial judgement is about allocating resources, not just generating activity.

For more on what happens before players even see a new game, see [why iGaming studios struggle with distribution](/articles/why-igaming-studios-struggle-with-distribution/).

The question every studio should ask

When the next promotional review lands on your desk, don't start with how much GGR increased.

Start with what actually changed.

Did you attract a different audience? Improve the operator's overall commercial performance? Generate profitable incremental revenue? Establish a stronger position for future releases?

Or did you simply buy a temporary increase in activity?

There's nothing wrong with using promotions to introduce games, support partnerships or secure visibility. They're valuable tools when used appropriately.

But promotional activity isn't the objective.

Profitable, sustainable growth is.

And if you can't demonstrate the relationship between the two, it's worth questioning what you're really achieving.
