For a game studio, distribution can look deceptively simple: make good games, sign an aggregator, reach operators.

In practice, those are three different challenges.

A studio can have strong content and a technical route into hundreds of operators, yet still struggle to secure launches, visibility and recurring revenue. The bottleneck is often less about getting connected and more about **getting commercially activated**.

Here are the problems I'd examine first.

## 1. Access is not activation

Signing an aggregator agreement is a step forward. It is not a launch plan.

The aggregator may distribute thousands of titles across many suppliers. Operators have their own release schedules, regulatory priorities, internal teams and promotional calendars.

So a studio needs to distinguish between:

- Operators technically reachable through an aggregator.
- Operators commercially contracted for its content.
- Operators that have completed the work required to go live.
- Operators that have launched and are generating meaningful activity.

Those are four separate numbers. Reporting one large headline figure as 'distribution' can conceal how little of the market is actually active.

## 2. The proposition isn't specific enough

A studio saying that its games are innovative, engaging and high quality is not giving an operator much to work with.

The operator wants to know how the content fits its audience. Is the portfolio suitable for the territory? Does it address a catalogue gap? What can the studio credibly say about early game performance, volatility, release cadence or promotional support?

A new studio may not have a long track record. That's fine. It does mean the proposition must be especially clear about what can be demonstrated and what remains unproven.

**A smaller, well-explained portfolio is often more useful in a commercial discussion than a large catalogue with no obvious audience fit.**

## 3. Commercial terms don't match the opportunity

Revenue share arrangements need to work for both parties.

A studio that gives away too much margin to secure access can discover that its successful games are economically underwhelming. An operator that is asked to accept terms out of line with the content's value may simply prioritise other suppliers.

The wrong incentives can create a second problem. An intermediary may be paid for introducing a contract, while the studio only benefits when games are genuinely live and producing revenue.

Commercial terms should reward the outcomes each party can influence, not just the signing ceremony.

## 4. Nobody owns the process after the deal

This is one of the least glamorous and most important issues.

A partnership might cross sales, legal, technical integration, compliance, product, account management and marketing teams. Without clear ownership, every small handover can create a delay.

Studios should track the journey from qualified opportunity to live operator as a managed commercial process. That includes the reason for every stalled launch.

Useful measures include time from contract to launch, active operators, revenue per active operator and the proportion of signed opportunities that become live relationships.

A signed deal that never launches is not a commercial win.

## 5. The launch gets more attention than the account

The first launch matters, but so do the months that follow.

Once a studio is live, someone still needs to understand performance, discuss upcoming releases, resolve operational issues and identify opportunities for broader distribution.

In a crowded supply market, operators are unlikely to give every studio equal attention. Account management provides a way to understand what is happening and make a grounded case for more.

I've seen across the supplier side that commercial performance is built through consistent partner ownership, not simply the volume of contracts signed.

## 6. The studio is trying to do everything at once

It's tempting for an ambitious studio to pursue every operator, aggregator and territory available.

But business development capacity is finite. So are integration, certification, support and release-management resources.

A more useful market plan ranks opportunities by expected commercial potential, delivery effort, suitability of the content and realistic time to revenue.

It is better to activate a focused group of operators properly than accumulate a long list of deals that never get past the pipeline.

## The useful question is 'what happens next?'

If I were reviewing a studio's distribution operation, I'd want a clear picture of the full funnel: access, commercial agreement, integration, launch, activity and growth.

Then I'd ask which stage is losing the most value, and why.

The answer might be better sales targeting. It might be pricing, account ownership, technical execution or the games themselves. Those are very different problems and need different solutions.
