*When revenue growth stalls, the instinct is often to recruit more business development people. But what if the problem isn't the size of your sales team?*

There's a familiar pattern in growing businesses.

Revenue starts to flatten. The pipeline isn't where it should be. New business takes longer to close, and existing customers aren't growing as quickly as expected.

The response? Hire another salesperson.

It sounds reasonable. More people generating opportunities should mean more revenue.

Except it doesn't always work that way.

I've spent much of my career building and leading commercial functions across iGaming and technology, from startups to established international businesses. One thing I've learned is that **sales capacity and commercial capability are not the same thing**.

And confusing the two can become an expensive mistake.

## The problem might not be your sales team

When a business isn't hitting its revenue targets, it's tempting to focus on activity.

How many meetings are being booked? How many proposals are going out? How many new opportunities are entering the CRM?

These are useful measures, but they don't tell the whole story.

A sales team can be extremely busy without being particularly effective.

Before assuming more headcount is the answer, I'd want to understand a few things:

- Are we targeting the right customers?
- Is our proposition genuinely differentiated?
- Are we pricing the product appropriately?
- Where are opportunities getting stuck?
- Are existing customers delivering their full commercial potential?
- Does the sales team have the tools, authority and support to close business?

If those questions don't have convincing answers, adding another salesperson may simply increase the cost of an already underperforming operation.

## Revenue problems often start before the first sales meeting

Consider a B2B technology supplier entering a new market.

The product is good. The founders know the industry. There's a sales team generating conversations, but deals are moving slowly.

Management concludes that the business needs a stronger salesperson with better industry relationships.

Perhaps it does.

But perhaps the target market was chosen because it looked attractive on paper rather than because the company had a credible route to winning customers.

Perhaps the product solves a problem that prospective customers don't consider urgent.

Or perhaps the business is targeting enterprise clients with lengthy procurement and integration requirements, while its financial plan assumes revenue will arrive within three months.

Those are commercial strategy problems.

Even the best salesperson will struggle to compensate for them.

## Your existing customers might be the biggest missed opportunity

New business gets attention. It's visible, exciting and easy to celebrate.

Account development rarely receives the same enthusiasm.

Yet in a recurring-revenue business, the commercial potential of existing relationships deserves just as much scrutiny as the new-business pipeline.

Are customers using everything they've contracted for? Are there opportunities to expand into new markets, brands or product categories? Do we understand their plans for the next twelve months?

And perhaps most importantly, is anyone genuinely responsible for growing those relationships?

I've seen the difference that structured account management can make. At Quickspin, I was responsible for a portfolio representing approximately £21 million in annual recurring revenue. Growing those relationships required much more than responding to customer requests.

It meant understanding the economics of each account, identifying opportunities, maintaining senior relationships and making sure commercial commitments translated into results.

Sometimes the most valuable new revenue opportunity is already sitting in your customer base.

## A bigger pipeline is not necessarily a healthier pipeline

Another common mistake is treating pipeline value as a proxy for future revenue.

A CRM containing £10 million of opportunities looks impressive.

But how much of it is genuinely qualified? How much has a realistic decision date? How much is dependent on factors outside the company's control?

And how much has been sitting at the same stage for six months because nobody wants to admit it's unlikely to close?

A pipeline should help management make decisions, not provide reassurance.

I'd rather see a smaller pipeline with credible qualification, clear next steps and realistic forecasting than an enormous collection of opportunities that never progress.

Commercial leadership means being prepared to challenge the numbers, including the ones everyone would prefer to believe.

## The founder bottleneck

This is particularly relevant to founder-led businesses.

In the early stages, the founder is often the best salesperson in the company. They know the product, understand the market and can make commercial decisions immediately.

That works until it doesn't.

As the business grows, the founder becomes involved in every significant negotiation, pricing exception, customer escalation and strategic decision.

The commercial team exists, but it cannot operate independently.

At this point, hiring another business development manager might help generate opportunities, but it won't remove the underlying dependency.

What the business may actually need is someone who can take ownership of the entire commercial function: strategy, people, processes, forecasting, partnerships and revenue performance.

Someone who can build a business that doesn't require the founder's intervention every time an important deal reaches the final stages.

## When should you actually hire more salespeople?

There are absolutely circumstances where additional sales capacity is the right investment.

But I'd want evidence of three things first.

**1. The proposition works.** We know who buys, why they buy and how to articulate the value.

**2. The commercial process is repeatable.** There is a reasonably predictable relationship between qualified opportunities, conversions and revenue.

**3. Existing capacity is genuinely constrained.** The team is missing achievable opportunities because it cannot cover the market effectively, rather than because the underlying process is inefficient.

When those foundations are in place, additional headcount can accelerate growth.

Without them, you're scaling uncertainty.

## Sometimes you need leadership before headcount

Not every growing business needs a full-time Chief Commercial Officer or Commercial Director.

A startup might need help defining its go-to-market strategy, building a pipeline and establishing its first partnerships.

A more established business might need someone to review its commercial operation, improve forecasting, develop its account management function or take temporary ownership of a struggling team.

In either case, experienced fractional or interim commercial leadership can provide an alternative to making a permanent senior hire before the business is ready.

But the remit matters.

An external commercial leader should be accountable for tangible outcomes, not simply producing a strategy document and disappearing.

The objective should be to leave the business with a stronger commercial operation than the one they inherited.

## Fix the engine before pressing the accelerator

Commercial growth isn't simply about persuading more people to buy something.

It's about understanding where revenue comes from, why customers choose you, how effectively the business converts opportunities and whether the commercial organisation can sustain growth.

Salespeople are an important part of that equation.

But they aren't the entire equation.

Before approving another recruitment budget, ask whether the business really needs more people selling, or whether it needs to get better at the commercial fundamentals.

The answer could save a considerable amount of money.

And, more importantly, unlock the growth you're actually looking for.
