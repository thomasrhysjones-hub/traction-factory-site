A studio has a game. An operator wants to offer it. Between those two businesses there is often a third: the aggregator.

That middle layer makes it easier for operators to access games from many studios through a smaller number of integrations. It can also handle reporting, technical connectivity and aspects of onboarding. For studios, aggregation can provide a route to operators they would otherwise struggle to reach.

It is useful infrastructure. It is not free infrastructure.

The commercial question is not whether aggregators add value. Many do. The question is **what that value costs, who pays for it and whether the economics still make sense when volume scales**.

## First, what is an aggregator being paid for?

An aggregator can provide a common technical route between a game studio and multiple operators. Depending on the arrangement, it may also support certification workflows, operational reporting, account coordination and commercial distribution.

However, an integration is not the same thing as distribution. Being listed in an aggregator's catalogue does not guarantee that operators will launch your games, feature them prominently or generate meaningful turnover.

That distinction matters for a new studio assessing its first major aggregator deal. The fee might buy access to a route to market, not guaranteed demand.

## How the money can flow

Commercial arrangements differ, but two broad models are worth understanding.

**Operator-funded aggregation:** The operator pays an aggregation fee, frequently linked to gaming activity or the value of the games consumed through the platform. The studio then has its own commercial agreement for the content.

**Studio-funded aggregation:** The studio agrees a commercial share with the operator, then pays an aggregator a proportion for the route through which the games are delivered.

Hybrid structures exist. There may also be minimum commitments, integration fees, hosting charges or commercial exceptions. Never assume that two headline percentages from different deals are directly comparable.

### A simple illustration

Imagine a commercial arrangement in which the operator's total content cost is **7% of the agreed revenue base**.

One possible structure is:

| Recipient | Illustrative share |
| --- | ---: |
| Game provider | 5% |
| Aggregator | 2% |
| Total operator content cost | 7% |

Another arrangement could have the operator contract with the provider for 7%, and the provider then pay 2% to the aggregator. The net provider position may look similar, but the contracting parties, invoicing, credit risk and operational responsibilities differ.

**These figures are illustrative, not an industry tariff or a quote for any particular aggregator.** Real agreements depend on operator scale, territory, bargaining power, product mix and the definition of the revenue base.

## Why the percentage alone can mislead

A two-percentage-point fee sounds straightforward. But before agreeing to it, work through the questions that determine the actual cost:

- **What is the revenue base?** Gross gaming revenue, net gaming revenue and other contract definitions are not interchangeable.
- **Who pays whom?** The answer changes invoicing, timing and exposure to unpaid amounts.
- **Is the fee uniform?** An aggregator may offer different economics for a large established operator and a small new entrant.
- **Are there fixed costs?** Minimum fees and one-off charges can make a seemingly low percentage expensive at small volumes.
- **What is included?** Technical delivery, operational support and commercial introductions are not necessarily one bundled service.
- **Can you move later?** Contractual exclusivity, integration dependencies and migration costs affect negotiating leverage.

The real metric is not merely the aggregator's percentage. It is the *total cost of reliable distribution for the amount of revenue the relationship creates*.

## What about a direct operator integration?

A direct integration can remove a layer of ongoing fees. It can also introduce engineering work, ongoing maintenance, certification demands, reporting requirements and support obligations.

A major studio with large volumes and a dedicated technical team can justify direct connections more easily than a small provider launching its first catalogue.

A sensible assessment compares the total cost of ownership over time, rather than declaring direct integration always cheaper or aggregation always better.

And even when a direct route makes financial sense, the operator may prefer an established aggregator for operational reasons.

## The question I'd ask before signing

For an operator: *What am I paying per unit of useful content, and what operational complexity does this route remove?*

For a studio: *How many operators can I realistically activate through this agreement, on what economics, and over what period?*

For an aggregator: *Are we making both sides easier to work with, or just adding another commercial layer?*

All three businesses can benefit from aggregation. But sustainable relationships usually come from being clear about who creates the value and who carries the cost.
