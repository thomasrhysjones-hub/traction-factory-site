AI demonstrations have a habit of making complicated things look remarkably simple.

Connect a few systems. Ask a question. Get a polished answer and a recommended next step. It looks like the commercial team has acquired an extremely competent colleague who doesn't need sleep.

The actual implementation is usually less glamorous. In iGaming, the difficult part is rarely getting a model to write a sensible paragraph. It's getting reliable information into the workflow, agreeing what that information means and making sure the output can be trusted.

**The real question is not whether AI can do something. It's whether it can do something useful, reliably, within the commercial and regulatory constraints of the business.**

## The industry isn't one connected dataset

Consider a game studio trying to understand which operator accounts deserve more attention.

Its games may be distributed directly to some operators and through aggregators to others. One integration might provide daily game-level figures. Another might provide monthly totals. Operator names, game IDs, currencies, time zones and the treatment of promotional adjustments may differ.

One dashboard reports GGR. Another focuses on net revenue after contractual deductions. A third mixes data from several jurisdictions with different reporting rules.

An AI system can summarise those figures beautifully. That doesn't make them comparable.

The same problem exists in affiliate acquisition, player analytics and commercial forecasting. The data often sits in different businesses, under different permissions, with different incentives to share it.

Before discussing models, ask: **who owns the data, what is its permitted use and are we comparing like with like?**

## A clever assistant cannot repair a weak process

Suppose an account management team doesn't consistently record next steps, account plans or operator conversations.

An AI assistant can draft a follow-up email. It can't reliably prioritise the most valuable next action if nobody has recorded the commercial context.

The basic process still matters:

- What triggers an account review?
- Who owns the opportunity or customer issue?
- What must be checked before a recommendation becomes a decision?
- Where is the action recorded and how is its outcome measured?

If these questions have no consistent answers, AI may simply accelerate inconsistency.

There is a useful distinction between **automating a task** and **improving the process that task sits inside**. The latter is where much of the value lives.

## Integration is a commercial problem, not just an IT problem

Connecting a CRM to a reporting system sounds technical. In practice, a good implementation also needs agreement about data access, system ownership and business logic.

Who is allowed to see operator-level profitability? Can salespeople access contract terms? Do supplier agreements permit information to be processed by an external AI provider? Which information can be retained, and for how long?

An integration may be technically possible but contractually or operationally inappropriate.

Businesses should decide the rules for data access and usage before building around them. That may mean starting with a narrower, better-governed workflow rather than connecting everything.

## The confident answer problem

Generative AI systems can produce plausible explanations that are wrong. The risk increases when the inputs are incomplete or the answer requires business knowledge the system has not been given.

Imagine a tool says an operator's revenue has fallen because a competitor secured better lobby placement. Revenue may indeed have fallen. The explanation may still be speculation.

A useful system should distinguish:

1. **Observed facts:** revenue declined against a defined baseline.
2. **Possible explanations:** visibility, seasonality, portfolio changes or technical issues.
3. **Missing evidence:** placement data, launch activity, promotional history or operator feedback.
4. **Recommended verification:** what a human should check next.

A tool that says "we don't know yet" is often more commercially valuable than one that invents certainty.

## Gambling adds another layer of responsibility

Commercial applications and player-facing applications don't carry identical risks.

Using AI to prepare a B2B meeting brief is one thing. Using it to influence player targeting, identify customer vulnerability or make decisions affecting individuals is another.

The [UK Gambling Commission's approach to AI](https://www.gamblingcommission.gov.uk/about-us/guide/page/the-commissions-approach-to-artificial-intelligence) highlights both opportunities and the need to manage risks. The [UK Information Commissioner's Office guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/what-are-the-accountability-and-governance-implications-of-ai) also makes clear the importance of accountability, data protection impact assessments and appropriate governance.

Requirements depend on the use case, jurisdiction and personal data involved. No sensible commercial team should treat an automated recommendation as a substitute for those checks.

## How I'd judge an AI pilot

I would start with a single workflow where there is already a clear owner and a measurable problem. For example: preparing operator account reviews.

Then check five things:

- **Inputs:** are account performance, commercial terms and correspondence accessible and consistent?
- **Baseline:** how long does a review take today, and how often does it identify a useful next action?
- **Output quality:** can the system cite its inputs, flag uncertainties and avoid inventing conclusions?
- **Human control:** who approves an action, and how are mistakes corrected?
- **Outcome:** did we save meaningful time, improve decision quality or generate incremental commercial value?

A 90-day pilot can be a sensible test window, but only if the underlying opportunity cycle is short enough to observe an outcome. Some B2B sales or distribution effects take longer.

If the system saves a little admin time but creates more checking work than it removes, that's worth knowing. If it produces attractive dashboards without changing decisions, that's also worth knowing.

## The bottom line

AI has credible applications across iGaming sales, account management, operations and analysis. But businesses won't get there by buying a platform and expecting it to discover the commercial strategy.

The advantage belongs to organisations that understand their processes, can trust their data and know what decision they want to improve.
